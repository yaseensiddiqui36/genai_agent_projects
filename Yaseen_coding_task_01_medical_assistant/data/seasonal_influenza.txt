Seasonal Influenza: Prevention and Care Overview

Overview
Seasonal influenza is a contagious respiratory illness caused by influenza A and B viruses.
It circulates most heavily in fall and winter months in temperate climates and can range from
mild to severe illness, occasionally resulting in hospitalization or death, particularly among
high-risk groups.

High-Risk Groups
Individuals at increased risk of severe influenza complications include adults 65 and older,
young children under 5, pregnant individuals, people with chronic conditions such as asthma,
diabetes, or heart disease, and people who are immunocompromised.

Prevention
The most effective way to prevent seasonal influenza is annual vaccination, ideally received
before the virus begins circulating widely in the community, ordinarily by the end of October.
Additional preventive measures include:
- Frequent handwashing with soap and water for at least 20 seconds, or using an alcohol-based
  hand sanitizer when soap is unavailable.
- Avoiding close contact with people who are sick, and staying home when experiencing symptoms
  to limit transmission.
- Covering coughs and sneezes with a tissue or the elbow rather than the hands.
- Routine cleaning and disinfecting of frequently touched surfaces.
- Improving indoor ventilation where possible.

Symptoms
Common symptoms include fever or feeling feverish, chills, cough, sore throat, runny or
stuffy nose, muscle or body aches, headaches, and fatigue. Some individuals, especially
children, may also experience vomiting and diarrhea.

Treatment
Most people with influenza recover with rest, hydration, and over-the-counter symptom relief.
Antiviral medications (e.g., oseltamivir) may be prescribed for people at higher risk of
complications or with severe illness, and are most effective when started within 48 hours of
symptom onset. Individuals with difficulty breathing, persistent chest pain, confusion, or
signs of dehydration should seek immediate medical attention.
